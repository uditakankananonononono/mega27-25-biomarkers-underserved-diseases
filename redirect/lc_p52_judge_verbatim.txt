Overall judgment: mostly sound intent, but several real statistical weaknesses remain

The preregistration discipline is strong (locked cohorts, no tuning, corrected permutation direction, external evaluation). The main vulnerabilities are small-sample inference, batch handling assumptions, and comparator choice.

(1) Platform-stratified z-scoring: adequate?

Partially, but not fully.

The approach is defensible, but the imbalance creates a real weakness:

Platform A has 3 cases / 7 controls. After within-platform z-scoring, the case distribution is estimated from only 3 samples. Any gene/module mean in that stratum has high variance.

If the platform effect is nonlinear or gene-specific beyond a simple location/scale shift, z-scoring does not remove it.

Stratified permutation preserves platform labels, which protects the p-value from obvious platform-label confounding, but it does not guarantee biological signal is independent of platform.

The biggest concern is that the training signal is partly identified from the platform split:

Cases are 30% of platform A but 53% of platform B.

A module that separates the platforms slightly can still look like a disease module after z-normalization.

This is not fatal, but I would want at least one preregistered sensitivity check:

train on platform B only (9 cases/8 controls), test whether direction survives;

or leave-one-platform-out as an exploratory robustness analysis.

(2) Beating the prior six-gene comparator |g| = 0.776: fair or stacked?

Mostly stacked unless the comparator was chosen before seeing this arc.

The problem is not benchmarking against a prior result; that is good. The problem is making it a hard win criterion when:

the comparator has only six genes,

it was optimized in some previous analysis,

its reported g may itself be upward-biased,

your signature is constrained by transportability and preregistration.

You are comparing:

a locked, transport-oriented discovery pipeline

against

a previously published/selected panel whose discovery process may not have had the same constraints.

That is not apples-to-apples.

A fairer rule would be:

report whether the new signature exceeds the comparator,

provide bootstrap confidence intervals for the difference,

avoid making "beats comparator" a pass/fail gate unless both were evaluated under identical locked conditions.

As written, leg iii risks turning a scientific comparison into a hurdle test.

(3) Is |g| > 0.8 too harsh with n=9 ME/CFS?

Yes, probably underpowered, but not scientifically unreasonable.

With:

ME/CFS n=9

Long COVID n=23

an observed Hedges g of 0.8 is a large effect. But the confidence interval will be very wide.

The issue is not that the threshold is "wrong"; the issue is that:

many real effects around g=0.5–1.0 will be impossible to distinguish reliably,

a true biological signal could fail because of sampling noise,

a winner will likely be a large-effect estimate with uncertainty.

So the bar makes the arc conservative, but the information gained is asymmetric:

failure tells you little;

success would be interesting.

I would not call it fatal, but interpret failures cautiously.

(4) Fatal flaws?

I do not see a fatal flaw that makes the entire experiment uninterpretable, but there are three serious risks:

Fatal-risk #1: Training set is too small for module selection

You have:

27 training samples

9 candidate modules

Even with fixed Hallmark-derived modules, selecting the one with p<0.05 and LOO sign stability introduces discovery uncertainty.

The two-sided permutation helps, but:

27 samples is extremely small for estimating a biological signature.

LOO sign stability is not independent validation.

This is the largest statistical weakness.

Fatal-risk #2: Disease contrast mismatch

Training:

PI-ME/CFS vs healthy

Evaluation:

Long COVID vs ME/CFS

These are not symmetric tasks.

The signature is trained to find ME/CFS-associated biology, not necessarily biology distinguishing ME/CFS from another post-infectious illness.

A module can pass E1 because it captures chronic illness/inflammation and fail E2 because Long COVID shares it.

That is scientifically informative, but the hypothesis must be framed carefully.

Fatal-risk #3: NanoString vs RNA-seq transport

This is probably the biggest biological generalization issue.

Training:

whole-transcriptome RNA-seq

Evaluation:

fixed 635-gene NanoString panel

Even with locked genes:

normalization differs,

dynamic range differs,

missing genes may alter module composition.

The 10+ gene availability rule helps, but you need to state whether the module score uses:

the same genes only, or

a redefined module after seeing NanoString availability.

Only the first is clean.

Answers in one line

Platform approach: acceptable but not sufficient; imbalance leaves residual batch-identification risk.

Comparator rule: likely stacked as a pass/fail criterion unless comparator was locked under identical conditions.

g>0.8: conservative and likely underpowered, but not invalid.

Fatal flaw: no single fatal flaw; biggest threats are tiny training n, selection optimism, and RNA-seq→NanoString transport validity.

The design is closer to a high-integrity falsification test than a discovery study. Its strongest possible result would be meaningful; its negative result would mostly show that this particular signature strategy/cohort pairing failed, not that ME/CFS/Long COVID lack molecular separation.
